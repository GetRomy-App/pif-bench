## 1. Personal and Professional Background

Agnes Gund — known universally in the art world as "Aggie" — was born in Cleveland, Ohio, on August 13, 1938, the second of six children. Her father, who had made a fortune in real estate and brewing, was president of the Cleveland Trust Company, then Ohio's largest bank; her mother, a homemaker, enrolled her in art classes at the Cleveland Museum of Art. The Gund family wealth was amassed through finance, real estate, and brewing.

Her formative art education came after her mother's 1954 death, when she enrolled at Miss Porter's School in Connecticut and encountered "a magical art history teacher who didn't just give you the artist's name and the date of the picture, she showed you how to look at artwork." She went on to earn a B.A. in History from Connecticut College in 1960 and a master's degree in art history from Harvard in 1980.

Her professional/board career was effectively a five-decade career as a nonprofit executive and arts patron. She joined the Museum of Modern Art's board in 1976, marking the beginning of a five-decade relationship with the museum, served as its president from 1991 to 2002, and helped expand the museum's educational programmes and increase its commitment to accessibility and diversity. She held the board presidency, an unpaid role, from 1991 to 2002. She had an instrumental role in helping raise funds for MoMA's $858m expansion, which was designed by the Japanese architect Yoshio Taniguchi and completed in 2004.

Her board affiliations extended across the U.S. cultural sector: the Cleveland Museum of Art, the Frick Collection, the Morgan Library and Museum, co-founder and Chair Emerita of the Center for Curatorial Leadership, and Honorary Trustee of YoungArts, Independent Curators International, and the Museum of Contemporary Art, Cleveland, plus the Aaron Diamond AIDS Research Center, the Andy Warhol Foundation, the Barnes Foundation, Chess in the Schools, the Foundation for Contemporary Arts, the Fund for Public Schools, the J. Paul Getty Trust, and the Robert Rauschenberg Foundation.

She was married twice, both ending in divorce, and is survived by four children including documentary filmmaker Catherine Gund and Anna Traggio.

## 2. Wealth Indicators and Asset Profile

**Source of wealth:** Inherited. In 1966, Gund's father died, leaving her tremendously wealthy. The family fortune, which Gund inherited upon her father's death in 1966, came from real estate and brewing.

**Family-level net worth:** Though she never made the Forbes billionaire list, Gund family net worth was estimated at $3.4 billion earlier last decade. This $3.4B figure is the broader Gund family, not Agnes individually — her brothers Gordon and George were partners in Gund Investment and owners of NHL and NBA franchises, so a substantial portion sits in their branches.

**Art collection as asset class:** This was her dominant non-financial asset. Most of the two thousand works comprising her collection — including those by Joseph Cornell, Philip Guston, Vija Celmins, Willem de Kooning, David Hammons, Eva Hesse, Yayoi Kusama, Ed Ruscha, Richard Serra, and Kara Walker — have been promised to museums. The 2017 Lichtenstein sale — $165 million for a single work — gives a sense of scale. Essentially all of her most valuable works that have not already been given are promised gifts to institutions, which is critical for prospect research: the collection is largely *off the table* for liquidation by heirs.

**Real estate:** Park Avenue residence in Manhattan (her primary home where she died) plus historical Cleveland family connections. Even by today's inflated standards of well-appointed Park Avenue residences, few exist where one could remove a prime Roy Lichtenstein and hardly miss it.

**Foundation assets (AG Foundation):** Recent annual grantmaking of approximately $8 million and total distributions exceeding $8 million in 2023. Per IRS Form 990-PF filings publicly available on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer (EIN 34-1606084), filings have been made annually through November 13, 2025 (covering FY 2024).

## 3. Philanthropic Giving History — Specific Amounts and Recipients

**Personal giving aggregate (1999–2025):** Agnes Gund has a substantial philanthropic record, including 592 contributions totaling an estimated $62,153,928 between 1999 and 2025. Her giving is concentrated in the arts and culture sector, primarily supporting The Museum of Modern Art (192), Barnes Foundation (63), and The Frick Collection (57). She has given to 38 institutions on record between the years of 1999-2025.

**Foundation giving cadence:** According to Sonia Lopez, the foundation's administrator and philanthropic advisor, each year the foundation makes 425 gifts totaling about $11 million, with approximately 90 percent of that supporting the arts (this 2008 figure was higher than the more recent ~$8M reported in 2023, suggesting a step-down). A 2014 NYT profile noted Gund donated $6 million to $7 million a year through her foundation with her "two areas of concentration" being cultural institutions and women's organizations.

**Headline transformative gifts:**

- **Art for Justice Fund — $100M (2017).** Gund made headlines when she sold Roy Lichtenstein's painting Masterpiece (1962) in 2017 to use the proceeds to establish the Art for Justice Fund, an initiative aimed at addressing the harms caused by mass incarceration in the US. The work was purchased by collector Steve Cohen for $165m, with Gund committing approximately $100m from the sale to launch the fund. Though Gund had previously donated artwork, including more than 900 pieces to the Museum of Modern Art alone, this was the first time she sold a work of art in direct support of social justice.

- **Studio in a School (1977 founding + ongoing).** In 1977, in response to New York City budget cuts to arts education in public schools, Gund founded Studio in a School, a nonprofit that engages professional artists as art instructors in public schools and community-based organizations. Studio in a School has provided visual art instruction led by professional artists to over one million students in over 800 schools and community-based organizations throughout the five boroughs of New York City. The organization is her single most enduring direct-service program. She has made it possible for 30,000 students, 90 percent of whom are under-privileged, to receive over 45,000 hours of art education a year.

- **Reproductive rights — $3.18M (2022).** In 2022, she sold Lichtenstein's Mirror #5 (1970) for $3.18 million, to benefit the work of two nonprofits fighting for reproductive rights after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade. Her own organization, the Art for Justice Fund, also contributed a matching donation.

- **Parrish Art Museum — $500K (2017).** Agnes Gund's recent $500,000 gift to the Parrish Art Museum in Water Mill, New York … will create and endow the Dorothy Lichtenstein ArtsReach Fund to "support programs designed to effect social change".

- **MoMA artwork donations.** She donated more than 250 artworks to MoMA over her lifetime, with other sources citing 700–900+ pieces given or funded. Plus annual cash giving — e.g., Agnes Gund donated $100,000 or more to The Modern Fund in 2022 at The Museum of Modern Art.

- **Cleveland Museum of Art.** Per Inside Philanthropy, Gund gave at least $10 million to the Cleveland Museum of Art over the years.

- **AG Foundation top recipients (per InfluenceWatch summary of 990s):** The AG Foundation's largest grants were made to the Cleveland Institute of Art, Chess in the Schools, the Foundation Fighting Blindness, and various museums including the MoMA, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Smithsonian.

- **Political giving (federal cycle, separate from foundation):** In 2021, she gave a $25,000 donation to Unite The Country, a political action committee aligned with President Joe Biden. She also gave $25,000 donations to the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee and Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee ahead of the 2022 midterm elections. Indicative of liquidity beyond the foundation.

- **Income-share commitment.** "I give away two-thirds of my income every year, and sometimes I have had to sell artwork to make good on my commitments."

## 4. Cause Areas and Giving Philosophy

**Stated philosophy:** Two anchor sentences capture it. Gund has said she believes that art "is a right, not a privilege," and this belief has been the driving force behind her philanthropic endeavors in the art world for over four decades. And on art's social function: "I think that now artists are really going to come to the fore when it comes to political and social causes. I think art can make a difference. I think art can help."

**Tier 1 — Visual arts, especially modern/contemporary.** Her dominant lifetime category. MoMA, MoMA PS1, Cleveland Museum of Art, Frick, Morgan, Barnes, Philadelphia Museum of Art, Foundation for Contemporary Arts.

**Tier 2 — Arts education for under-resourced public-school students.** Studio in a School is the signature program. The foundation operates on a relationship-based model tied to Agnes Gund's personal connections and board service across major cultural institutions; it includes Education & Youth Programs: Including Chess in the Schools.

**Tier 3 — Criminal justice reform and racial justice.** Art for Justice Fund is the marquee vehicle. Motivation was personal: Six of her grandchildren are black, and she speaks of tragedies like the shooting death of 12-year-old Tamir Rice by police in her native Cleveland the way only a grandmother can.

**Tier 4 — Women artists, women's leadership, reproductive rights.** A consistent through-line. She had specifically pushed for the acquisition of works by women and artists of colour, who remained underrepresented in the collection.

**Tier 5 — Health (familial connections).** Gund provided ongoing financial support to the Foundation Fighting Blindness, an organization dedicated to funding research on retinal diseases, motivated by familial connections as her brother Gordon Gund served as co-founder. Plus AIDS research and Breast Cancer Research Foundation.

**Tier 6 — Democratic political giving.** Substantial, separate from the foundation. The New York Times — in an article entitled "Is Agnes Gund the Last Good Rich Person?" — described Gund as "a virtual A.T.M. for Democratic politicians".

**Style markers (useful for any successor solicitation):** Hands-on, low-PR, allergic to limelight, long-relationship-driven, friendships with artists and curators, donor-organizer role (recruiting other collectors). She personally championed using art-asset liquidation to fund mission rather than pure cash gifts — a model she'd want institutional successors to perpetuate.

## 6. Recommended Ask Amount and Engagement Strategy

Given Agnes is deceased, the actionable strategy is for her **successor entities and family network**.

**Primary recommendation: target the AG Foundation, anchor ask $100,000.**

Rationale: this sits at the upper-mid range of their typical grant size ($25K median, $500K ceiling), is high enough to be strategically meaningful but well within precedent, and matches the foundation's 90% arts-skewed portfolio. **Engagement sequence:**

1. *Identify a credible bridge.* The foundation runs on relationships. Map the boards of Studio in a School, MoMA Department of Education, Center for Curatorial Leadership, and the Fund for Public Schools — anyone on the program's existing board or advisory council who served alongside Agnes on any of these is the highest-leverage introduction route.
2. *Lead with Catherine Gund.* She is an Emmy-nominated documentary filmmaker, producer, and activist who founded Aubin Pictures in 1996. Her documentaries focus on social justice themes, including "Aggie" (2020) about her mother Agnes Gund, and chairs the George Gund Foundation. A program that frames arts education as racial-equity and social-justice work — not just enrichment — will resonate more with Catherine than with the historical foundation.
3. *Frame the gift around legacy.* Position the program as an extension of, not competitor to, Studio in a School — e.g., "fills a gap Studio in a School doesn't reach" (different age range, different art form, different borough, different model). 4. *Pre-position with Studio in a School first.* Get on a panel, co-host an event, run a pilot in collaboration. Demonstrated credibility with Studio's leadership (Hasna Muhammad, Chair) is the cheapest pathway to AG Foundation legitimacy.
5. *Cultivation timing.* The foundation is in transition. The 12–18 months following her September 2025 death are when trustees are establishing their own giving identity — this is a meaningful window before patterns calcify.

**Secondary asks worth running in parallel:**

## 7. Key Connection Points and Potential Red Flags

**Connection points (warm leads in the network):**

- **Anna Traggio (daughter)** — AG Foundation trustee; an assistant coach for The Hotchkiss School's girls' varsity lacrosse team.

- **Brooke Garber Neidich, Amanda and Glenn Fuhrman, Maria Hummer-Tuttle, Daniel Loeb** — collector friends she recruited to Art for Justice.

**Potential red flags:**

1. **Studio in a School is the elephant in the room.** A new NYC public-school arts-education nonprofit asking the Gund estate for funding is, on its face, asking them to fund a competitor to the founder's flagship organization. You must have a clear differentiation story (different art form, different population, different methodology) or the ask will read as misaligned.
2. **No unsolicited proposals.** The foundation does not accept unsolicited proposals. Cold inbound has near-zero hit rate; this is a relationship-only foundation.
3. **Political alignment skew.** AG Foundation giving has historically been progressive. Programs framed as politically neutral or coded conservative will face headwinds — and conversely, very explicit progressive framing can polarize boards if your nonprofit serves a mixed political constituency.
4. **Estate uncertainty.** Agnes Gund, best known for her deep support and involvement with New York City's Museum of Modern Art, passed away in 2025, leaving much about her foundation's future in question. Don't plan multi-year asks against an entity whose strategic direction is genuinely unresolved.
5. **"Founder dependency" risk in the foundation itself.** With Agnes gone, the relationships that drove giving (her personal involvement on 23+ boards) are severed. The new trustees may radically reshape priorities — Catherine, in particular, may push more aggressively into social-justice documentary, criminal justice, and racial equity, and away from traditional arts-institution support. Track the FY2024 and FY2025 990s as they're released for the first signal.
6. **Don't overweight the $3.4B family-wealth headline.** That figure is the *combined* Gund family across her brothers' branches, not Agnes's individual estate. Her personal liquid wealth was meaningfully smaller — and most of her remaining art is already promised away. The realistic post-death pool is the AG Foundation's existing endowment plus whatever bequest flows in, not anywhere near $3.4B.
7. **Sourcing caveat.** Several of the most useful single-source figures (PatronView's $62M / 592-gift aggregate, the "two-thirds of income" line, family net worth estimate) come from secondary aggregators or her own self-reporting in interviews, not from primary 990 cross-tabulation. If you're putting numbers into a Rōmy donor profile, weight the AG Foundation 990s (publicly available via ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer, EIN 34-1606084) as primary; treat the rest as supporting context.
