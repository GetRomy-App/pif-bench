% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
We present PIF-Bench v2, a benchmark of how often AI systems do nonprofit development (fundraising) work \emph{completely} right. It replaces PIF-Bench v1, which scored donor-research reports from 0 to 100 on seven hand-weighted dimensions in a single holistic pass and could not be audited. Version~2 adopts the design of Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark: 25 tasks, each with an inline rubric of binary pass/fail criteria written before any system was run (331 criteria in all), graded one criterion at a time by judges blind to which system wrote the work, with the share of tasks on which \emph{every} criterion passed as the headline. Twenty \emph{packet} tasks give the system a closed set of synthetic records and policies across six areas of development work---prospect research, foundations and grants, gift compliance, major gifts, donor data, and finance and governance---so that every right answer is known by construction. Five \emph{live} tasks ask for donor research on real philanthropists and are graded against facts true on a fixed date, each with a primary source. Every task ships a correct and a flawed fixture, and judges are trusted only as far as they grade those correctly: a three-family LLM panel reached 100\% on the fixtures once four rubric defects it exposed were repaired, and the blind agent judge used for this release returned all 662 fixture verdicts correctly and agreed with an independent second judging on 99.6\% of 898 verdicts ($\kappa = 0.987$). On the packet track, GPT-6.1 Sol, Muse Spark~1.3 and Claude Opus~5.5 completed 95\% of tasks with every criterion met, against 70\% (Gemini~3.8 Flash), 65\% (MiniMax~M3) and 55\% (Gemini~3.1 Pro). On the live track, re-grading the donor reports that four products wrote in April 2026, no product met every criterion for any prospect; the share of criteria met was 83\% for Claude.ai, 74\% for \romy, 67\% for Gemini and 60\% for ChatGPT. The ordering of the top two reverses v1's published result for the same reports. Every task, rubric, deliverable, verdict and line of scoring code is released.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} benchmark, large language models, LLM-as-a-judge, rubric evaluation, prospect research, nonprofit fundraising, hallucination

% ── 1. Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A development office runs on work whose errors are expensive in a particular way. A prospect researcher who merges two people with the same name, a gift officer who visits a donor who died three weeks earlier, a receipt that overstates what a gala guest may deduct, a memo that moves a restricted endowment into operations: each is the kind of mistake that damages the relationship the work exists to build, and each is now within reach of an AI system asked to help. Nonprofits are adopting such systems quickly, and they need a way to know how often the work comes back right.

In April 2026 we published PIF-Bench (``Prospect Intelligence Fidelity''), a benchmark of AI donor research \citep{lijo2026pifv1}. It scored each system's report on five prominent philanthropists from 0 to 100 on seven weighted dimensions and placed our own system, \romy, first. On reflection, that design could not carry the weight we put on it. The dimension scores were holistic judgments by a single AI model and could not be audited; one dimension rewarded our own sixteen-section report format; another scored capacity estimates against ``verified actuals'' that do not exist for a living donor; parts of the answer key were written after reading the systems' outputs; and the judge shared a model family with one contestant while the benchmark's authors built another (\Cref{sec:v1}).

This paper presents PIF-Bench v2, rebuilt from the ground up on the design of Harvey's Legal Agent Benchmark \citep{harvey2026lab}, the open benchmark for AI on legal work. Its contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A benchmark of nonprofit development work} (\Cref{sec:design}): 25 tasks in two tracks---20 closed-world \emph{packet} tasks whose answers are known by construction, and 5 \emph{live} tasks on real philanthropists graded against dated, sourced facts---with 331 binary criteria fixed before any system ran.
  \item \textbf{A grading protocol in which every number can be checked} (\Cref{sec:grading}): one judge call per criterion, blinding, judges calibrated on planted errors, and grade records that tie every verdict to the exact rubric, deliverable text, judge model and grading protocol that produced it.
  \item \textbf{Results} (\Cref{sec:results}) for six models on the packet track and for four products' April 2026 reports on the live track, re-graded blind.
  \item \textbf{A frank account of how the first version went wrong} (\Cref{sec:v1,sec:discussion}), offered as a case study in benchmarks built by an interested party.
\end{enumerate}

We acknowledge at the outset that \romy, which the author works on, is one of the systems graded, and that the judge used for this release's leaderboards is a Claude model while Claude systems are among those graded. \Cref{sec:limitations} discusses both; the release is designed so that neither has to be taken on trust.

% ── 2. Background ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Domain benchmarks.} General-capability benchmarks such as MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021measuring} and HELM \citep{liang2023holistic} measure breadth; domain benchmarks such as MedQA \citep{jin2021disease}, LegalBench \citep{guha2024legalbench} and FinBen \citep{xie2024finben} measure competence in a profession. Long-form factuality methods such as FActScore \citep{min2023factscore} verify atomic claims in generated biographies, which is close to one part of prospect research but says nothing about the procedural work that surrounds it---applying a receipting policy, a capacity formula or a spending rule.

\paragraph{Rubric benchmarks for professional work.} Harvey's BigLaw Bench \citep{harvey2024biglaw} graded legal work products against task-specific rubrics that award points for what a good answer must contain and deduct them for errors and hallucinations. Its successor, the Legal Agent Benchmark (LAB) \citep{harvey2026lab}, released in May 2026, moved to equally weighted binary criteria written into each task, graded criterion by criterion by two LLM judges, with the headline an \emph{all-pass} rate: a task scores only if every criterion is met, on the reasoning that a diligence memo that misses one material issue is not 95\% useful but wrong. Leading systems pass individual criteria at high rates yet complete only a minority of tasks in full. PIF-Bench v2 adopts this design and adapts it to nonprofit work.

\paragraph{LLMs as judges.} Using LLMs to grade LLM output scales evaluation but introduces biases, including position and verbosity effects and a preference for a judge's own outputs \citep{zheng2023judging,panickssery2024llm}. Binary criteria with explicit pass and fail conditions narrow the room for such effects; blinding and cross-family panels reduce it further; and grading known-answer fixtures measures what remains.

% ── 4. Design ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Tasks and tracks}

A task is a directory holding a \texttt{task.json} (instructions, the scenario date, the deliverable's file name and the rubric), a \texttt{documents/} folder the system reads, an optional \texttt{references/} folder only the judge reads, and a \texttt{fixtures/} folder with a correct and a flawed deliverable. Tasks belong to one of two tracks, which are always reported separately.

\paragraph{Packet track (headline).} Twenty tasks give the system a packet of synthetic records---CRM exports, county property records, Form~990-PF excerpts, contact reports, broker transfer notices, board minutes---together with the organization's own policies, a scenario date, and a request written as a supervisor would write it. Every person and organization is fictional, so a system can answer only by reading. Every rule a task applies is written into the packet---a capacity formula, a receipting policy citing IRC~\S170(f)(8) and \S6115, an excerpt of the Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act, a paraphrase of the Schedule~A public support test---so that the right answer follows from the files and is not a matter of opinion. \Cref{tab:areas} summarizes the areas; \Cref{app:tasks} lists every task.

\paragraph{Live track.} Five tasks reuse v1's prompts for MacKenzie Scott, Reed Hastings, Agnes Gund, Laurene Powell Jobs and Robert F.~Smith, each paired with a requesting organization (\Cref{app:prompts}). The system receives only the prompt and does its own research. Criteria test facts true on 25 April 2026, the date the v1 reports were collected, and each carries one or more primary-source URLs (SEC filings, Forms~990-PF, recipients' announcements, a Department of Justice release, published foundation policies). A judge-only fact sheet records the verified facts with tolerances where sources disagree. The live rubrics were researched and written by authors who never saw any system's output.

\subsection{Criteria}

Each criterion has an identifier, a title and a \texttt{match\_criteria} text that states both a \pass{} and a \fail{} condition, naming the exact figure, date, person or rule and, wherever possible, the likely wrong answers. For example, from the stock-gift task:

\begin{quote}\small
\pass{} if the Marchetti gift is valued at \$25,068.00: 400 shares $\times$ \$62.67, the mean of QPXI's high (\$63.48) and low (\$61.86) on 2026-01-02. \fail{} if the gift value is any other figure---such as \$24,820.00 (the \$62.05 closing price), \$24,584.00 (the 2025-12-29 mean), \$24,976.00 (the 2025-12-31 mean), \$25,240.00 (the sale principal) or \$25,215.00 (sale proceeds net of commission).
\end{quote}

Criteria are equally weighted. None grades format, length or tone. Following LAB, rubric authors were asked to include only what a supervising development officer would actually check before the work went out. Each packet task has two to four \emph{traps}---a namesake, a property sold after the vendor's data date, a gift outside a five-year window, a donor-list range, a donor-advised fund grant asked to pay for gala seats, a check postmarked in the prior fiscal year, a board member's undisclosed interest---each tied to a criterion. A criterion that tests invention (``states nothing the files do not support'') lists every packet file as a reference and says explicitly that arithmetic slips and misapplied rules are graded by the task's other criteria; calibration showed that without both, judges read a single error as two (\Cref{sec:calibration}).

\subsection{Fixtures and integrity}

Every task ships a reference deliverable that passes every criterion and a flawed deliverable with three to six planted errors, with an \texttt{expected.json} listing exactly the criteria those errors fail. Everything else in the flawed deliverable stays correct, so a judge must both catch the planted errors and pass the correct work. An offline integrity check, run in continuous integration, enforces the rubric conventions: sequential identifiers, explicit \pass{} and \fail{} conditions, existing reference files, an evidence URL on every live criterion, a full packet for every invention criterion, no documents on live tasks, and fixtures consistent with the rubric. Every figure in a packet rubric was recomputed by an independent script from the packet alone.

% ── 5. Grading ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Grading and Scoring}
\label{sec:grading}

\subsection{Judging}

A judge sees the assignment, the deliverable, the reference files a criterion names, and that one criterion, and returns a verdict with two to four sentences of reasoning quoting the deliverable (\Cref{app:judge}). One call per criterion keeps criteria from colouring each other and makes every verdict traceable. The standard panel has one judge from each of three model families; a criterion passes on a strict majority of the whole panel, and a judge error is never a pass. Before a judge reads a deliverable, names of AI systems and vendors are replaced with ``[the assistant]'' and per-product citation tracking parameters (\texttt{utm\_source=\ldots}) are removed; house formats can still reveal a system, which is one reason criteria test content rather than style.

\subsection{What a grade is tied to}

Every grade records the rubric hash (instructions, criteria and files as the judge saw them), the judge model, a hash of the deliverable's exact text, and a grading-protocol hash covering the judge prompt, message template and blinding rules. A grade that no longer matches any of these is redone, never reused, and the report ignores it until it is. Every deliverable records the hash of the prompt it was produced from; a deliverable produced from an earlier version of a task's documents is regenerated rather than graded. An independent code review of the harness found five ways a published number could have been silently wrong---among them grades outliving a corrected deliverable and missing deliverables dropping out of the denominator---and each was reproduced, fixed and covered by a test before any result was published.

\subsection{External judges}

People, or agents outside the harness, grade through an export--import path. Each deliverable becomes a Markdown grading packet under a random identifier; the identifier-to-system key is kept apart and never shown to a judge. Packets are dealt into batches so that no judge sees two answers to the same task---a judge cannot compare systems, only apply the rubric---and are written in shuffled order with a common timestamp. Each task's fixtures are mixed into the pool unmarked, so the judges' accuracy on known answers is measured under exactly the conditions in which they grade real work. A verdict file that misses, repeats or invents a criterion, or that arrives for a deliverable changed since its packet was made, is rejected whole.

\subsection{Scores}

The headline is the \textbf{all-pass rate}: the share of tasks on which every criterion passed. Two diagnostics accompany it: \textbf{criteria met}, the mean over tasks of each task's passed fraction, and the \textbf{red-line failure rate}, the share of tasks with at least one red-line criterion failed. Attempts are averaged within a task before tasks are averaged. Every system is scored on the same set of tasks: a system with no deliverable for a task scores zero on it, so omitting a hard task cannot help, and a system with ungraded work, a judge error or a stale deliverable is reported as not ranked. The all-pass rate carries a 95\% Wilson interval \citep{wilson1927probable}; criteria met carries a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval over tasks \citep{efron1993introduction} with a fixed seed. Percentages are reported as whole numbers; with tens of tasks, more precision would be false.

\subsection{Judge quality}
\label{sec:judgequality}

Three measures accompany every result. \emph{Calibration} is a judge's accuracy against the fixtures' expected verdicts, separating false passes (a planted error let through) from false fails and errors. \emph{Agreement} is reported as the share of unanimous verdicts, Fleiss' $\kappa$ \citep{fleiss1971measuring} for the panel and Cohen's $\kappa$ \citep{cohen1960coefficient} for each pair. \emph{Self-preference} for a judge is how much more it passes its own family's system than the judges of other families do, minus the same quantity on neutral systems---those whose family has no judge on the panel---so that one biased judge cannot make another look biased.

% ── 6. Setup ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Packet-track systems.} Six models were run through the Vercel AI Gateway on 10 October 2026: Claude Opus~5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini~3.1 Pro, Gemini~3.8 Flash, MiniMax~M3 and Muse Spark~1.3. Each received the same short preamble (\Cref{app:judge}), the scenario date, the packet's files and the request; output was capped at 32{,}000 tokens; sampling used provider defaults; each task was attempted once. Generation cost \$9.42 in total, from \$0.021 per task (MiniMax~M3) to \$0.191 (Claude Opus~5.5). One generation returned no text after spending its output budget on reasoning and was retried once, successfully. Three deliverables reached the output cap and were graded as written: one by Gemini~3.8 Flash and two by MiniMax~M3.

\paragraph{Live-track systems.} The live track grades the reports that four products wrote on 25 April 2026 for v1---\romy{} (Deep Research), Claude.ai (Opus~4.7 Adaptive), ChatGPT (GPT-5.3) and the Gemini app (3.1 Pro)---collected by hand from each product's interface. They were split from the files v1 published, verbatim except for the echoed prompt, with line-level provenance. The v1 outputs for MacKenzie Scott were not kept, so that task is unscored; four prospects remain. The Claude.ai reports refer to \romy{} by name although the prompts never did, which suggests that run had \romy{} project context; it is reported but should not be read as a clean product run.

\paragraph{Rubric calibration.} While the rubrics were built, every task's fixtures were graded by a gateway panel of Claude Sonnet~5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol and Gemini~3.1 Pro, and by a budget panel of Claude Haiku~5.5, GPT-6~Luna and Gemini~3.8 Flash. Their misses drove the rubric repairs described in \Cref{sec:calibration}.

\paragraph{Grading this release.} The leaderboards were graded by a blind agent judge: fresh instances of Claude Opus~5.5 running in Claude Code, working through the external-judge path. The 186 grading packets---120 packet-track deliverables, 16 live-track reports and all 50 fixtures, unmarked---were dealt into 19 batches of nine or ten, one agent per batch, each instructed to read only its own packets and to grade every criterion independently. The panel file, brief, batch plan, key and every verdict file are published.

% ── 7. Results ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Judges}
\label{sec:calibration}

\Cref{tab:calibration} reports judge accuracy on the fixtures. On the first pass the three-family panel's majority verdict was 99.5\% accurate with no false passes. Every miss was a judge \emph{failing} correct work, and every one traced to a rubric defect rather than a judge error: invention criteria that read a misapplied rule as an invented one, a criterion that restated another's requirement, a flawed fixture whose planted error legitimately tripped a second criterion, and invention criteria that showed the judge only part of the packet. After repairs, the panel's majority was 100\% accurate, and on the sixteen tasks unchanged by the final repair all three flagship judges were individually 100\% accurate. The blind agent judge used for the leaderboards returned all 662 fixture verdicts correctly under blind, mixed conditions. An earlier, independent blind judging of the 66 live and fixture deliverables, made before citation tracking parameters were stripped, agreed with the final judging on 894 of 898 criterion verdicts (99.6\%, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.987$); in all four disagreements the final judging was the stricter.

The contradictions were concrete: Netflix ``co-founded in 1998'' (Gemini; it was 1997), a net worth of ``\$21--24+ billion'' for Laurene Powell Jobs (ChatGPT; about \$13.9 billion on the day), Monumental Sports described as a current holding after its December 2025 sale (Gemini), a 2016 Cornell gift of ``\$30M'' (Claude.ai; it was a \$50 million commitment), Reed Hastings's stake as ``approximately 4.8\%'' of Netflix and Robert F.~Smith's net worth as ``\$28.5 billion'' (\romy; under 1\% and \$10 billion respectively). Two products crossed a substantive red line. ChatGPT, having noted that Agnes Gund had died, still set a \$5--10 million ask addressed to her, as v1 had also found. Gemini recommended approaching Emerson Collective's program staff with a proposal, although Emerson publishes a policy of accepting no unsolicited submissions. \romy{} recommended an unprompted briefing to the trustees of a foundation that states it gives only to preselected organizations, and described Smith's 2020 non-prosecution agreement as a civil settlement. Every product failed the criteria on the AG Foundation's grants to Studio in a School and on The City Fund, Hastings's principal charter-school vehicle launched in 2018 with the Arnolds---both documented and directly relevant to the requester.

% ── 8. Discussion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The packet track saturates at the top.} Three models missed one criterion in 259 each. That the leading frontier models handle well-specified, single-deliverable development tasks almost perfectly is itself a useful result for nonprofits, but it means this version of the packet track cannot separate the leaders. LAB's leaders complete a minority of its tasks; our tasks are shorter, every rule is in the packet, and there is a single deliverable. Harder tasks---multi-step, multi-document, with deliverables that interact---are the clearest next step.

\paragraph{Research on real people is where systems fail.} On the live track, where systems must find and reconcile facts rather than read them, no product delivered a report that met every check, and every product stated something that contradicted the record on the day. The errors were not exotic: a wrong founding year, a superseded net worth, a divested stake presented as current. For fundraisers, the implication is practical: AI research on a prospect should be treated as a draft to verify before it reaches a gift officer, whichever product produced it.

\paragraph{How v1 misled.} Re-grading the same reports changes the conclusion of v1 in two ways. The ranking at the top reverses once format is no longer scored, and the level falls once ``accuracy'' means specific checks that either pass or fail rather than an impression on a 100-point scale. Both effects ran in the direction of the benchmark's authors. We think the lesson generalizes: a benchmark built by a vendor should fix its rubric before running anything, score content rather than style, publish every verdict, and make its judges prove themselves on planted errors.

% ── 9. Limitations ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{A single-family judge.} The leaderboards were graded by Claude Opus~5.5 agents, and Claude systems are graded: Claude Opus~5.5 ties for first on the packet track, and Claude.ai leads the live track. Blinding, binary criteria that name their figures, separation of answers to the same task, and calibration on unmarked fixtures (662 of 662 correct) limit the room for favouritism, and every verdict carries its reasoning; but a single-family judge cannot measure self-preference \citep{panickssery2024llm}. On the packet track, Claude Opus~5.5 is level with two systems from other families rather than ahead of them. Re-grading this release with the three-family panel is one command for anyone with gateway access; we have not yet done so.

\paragraph{The April live data.} The Claude.ai run appears to have had \romy{} project context, the Scott reports were not kept, and each product was run once by hand. Four prospects support wide intervals only.

\paragraph{Authorship of tasks.} Packet tasks, rubrics, fixtures and live fact sheets were drafted with AI assistance under the author's direction and then verified: every packet figure was recomputed independently, every rubric was tested against planted errors, and live facts carry primary sources. Tasks drafted this way may still share blind spots with the systems they test, and no practising development officer has yet reviewed them.

\paragraph{LLM judges are not people.} Calibration measures accuracy on known answers; it cannot prove that judges generalize to every deliverable. Human review of a sample of verdicts is the next validation step.

\paragraph{Single attempts and scenario rules.} Each model attempted each task once; run-to-run variance is unmeasured. Packet rules are the organizations' own policies; a reader may disagree with a rule but can check whether a deliverable applied it.

\paragraph{Developer conflict.} The benchmark is maintained by \romy, which builds one of the graded systems. The design choices in \Cref{tab:v1} and the release of every artifact are intended to make the results checkable without trusting the maintainer.

% ── 10. Ethics ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

Live-track prospects are public philanthropists, and their rubrics use only public, primary-source facts; a speculative inference about one prospect that a draft fact sheet contained was removed before release. Packet tasks use fictional people throughout; their ethics-screening task tests whether a system \emph{omits} medical, family-court and minor-child information that a research-ethics policy forbids, a behaviour we consider essential in tools that aggregate information about individuals. Benchmark results about named commercial products are published with their caveats and with the raw material needed to contest them.

% ── 11. Future work ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

We plan (1) harder, multi-step packet tasks with several interacting deliverables; (2) \romy's own product on the packet track, and fresh, documented live-track runs for every product including MacKenzie Scott; (3) multiple attempts per task to measure variance; (4) a three-family re-grade of every published run and human review of disputed verdicts; and (5) tasks contributed by practising prospect researchers, development officers and controllers, for which the repository includes an authoring guide.

% ── 12. Conclusion ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

PIF-Bench v2 asks a simple question of AI systems doing nonprofit development work---how often is the work completely right?---and answers it with rubrics fixed in advance, judges that must first grade planted errors correctly, and a published record of every verdict. On well-specified tasks with every rule in hand, the best current models are nearly flawless and the rest are not. On research about real people, no product yet produces a report that survives a careful check, and the first version of this benchmark said otherwise. Both findings are released with everything needed to test them.

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:judge}

\paragraph{Candidate preamble (packet track).}
\begin{quote}\small
You are assisting the staff of a nonprofit organization with development (fundraising) work.

The files for this assignment are provided below. Treat them as the complete record available to you: base every statement about people, organizations, gifts and figures on these files, and cite the file path (for example [documents/deed-records.md]) for each fact you take from them. Where the files do not answer something the assignment asks, say so plainly instead of estimating or guessing.

Return the deliverable itself as a single Markdown document, with no preamble and no closing offer of further help.
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Judge instructions (harness judges).}
\begin{quote}\small
You grade one rubric criterion at a time for a benchmark of AI systems that do nonprofit development (fundraising) work. You see the assignment the system was given, the deliverable it produced, and one criterion. Decide whether the deliverable satisfies that criterion.

Rules: (1) Grade only the criterion in front of you. The deliverable's other strengths and weaknesses do not matter. (2) The criterion's PASS/FAIL text is the standard. Do not substitute your own knowledge or opinion for it. (3) Match substance, not wording. Different phrasing of the required point passes; formatting differences in numbers (\$1.2M vs \$1,200,000) do not matter. (4) Numbers must equal the criterion's figures unless the criterion states a tolerance. (5) Where the criterion says FAIL if something appears, look through the whole deliverable for it. (6) If the deliverable states the required point and also contradicts it elsewhere, it fails. (7) Reference files, when given, are ground truth for facts. (8) The deliverable has been blinded: names of AI systems read ``[the assistant]''. Ignore that.

Respond with JSON only: \{"reasoning": "\ldots", "verdict": "pass" | "fail"\}
\end{quote}

The blind agent judges received the same grading rules, adapted to a batch of packet files, and were instructed to read only their own packets; the brief is published with the verdicts.
